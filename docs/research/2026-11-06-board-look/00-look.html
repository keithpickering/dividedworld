The 2003 board look

Round one of the board world's design boards. Before any screen is drawn, this round settles the house look the screens share: how strict the black and white stays, what the buttons are, and whether themes beyond light and dark are part of the design. The glass's boards were drawn the same way, a look first and the screens after.

Everything below the switcher is one board, drawn at the 1440 screen's 1200px column and the 1.25 type scale the build uses, with the demo world's real content. Switch the look, the mode, the page and the buttons; the panel under the frame names the tokens each look sets.

As built today

Shot from main (354dbc4) on the in-memory demo, light index and dark board. The structure is right: the index, the thread table, the postbit column. What it lacks is hierarchy inside the tables.

  • Every rule is the same rule. The outer frame, the column heads, and the lines between rows are one 1px line, so the eye gets no anchor. Period boards gave the column heads and the category a band of their own.
  • Three things look identical. The NEW chip, the avatar's letter box, and the Start a thread button are each a black-bordered box. A reader can't tell the control from the label.
  • Rows don't alternate, so a 169-post thread list is a field of lines. Alternating rows were the one thing every 2003 skin did.
  • The page stops, it doesn't end. No footer, nothing under Who is online. Period boards closed with a footer that said where you were.
  • Pure white on pure black vibrates in dark mode. The 1px white rules on #000 shimmer at a 21:1 edge. Dark skins of the era sat on charcoal, not black.

The board, three ways

Strict keeps the spec's two values and gets its hierarchy from period structure alone: reverse-video column heads, a double outer rule, 1-bit folder icons. Silver Recommended lets the house use grays. That's what a kid with a free afternoon and Photoshop 7 actually shipped in 2003: a gradient GIF behind the column heads, two alternating row grays, a table drawn with cellspacing="1" so the gaps are the grid. It's still black and white, and the only color on the page is still the game's pieces.

Image buttons

Switch Buttons above to compare. Plain is the build. Bevel is the browser's own 1999 button, the one a skin with no budget kept. Chrome is the Photoshop 7 button: gradient overlay with a hard midline, a 1px stroke, an inner highlight, a soft drop shadow and an outer glow on the lettering. It reads as a GIF because that's every layer style a 2003 kid turned on.

All three are real text in a real <button>, so they hold the forum's own accessibility: labels a screen reader reads, contrast measured on the face, a focus ring, and translation without new art. In Chrome the label is set in lowercase, as the GIFs usually were; the accessible name keeps the copy's own case. The 80×15 badges in the footer use a pixel face (Silkscreen) as art. They're decorative and hidden from assistive tech, and every word on them is true.

More themes

What exists. A theme is already a stylesheet of --kb-* tokens and nothing else (tools/check-theme.mjs refuses anything more). An instance lists its themes as ThemeOptions, each saying whether it holds both modes or only one, and Settings' row already appends every instance theme after the house's two. So the plumbing for a third theme is there. Divided World just lists one.

What's missing. First, vocabulary. The tokens name about eleven chrome colors (fg, bg, rule, muted, links). None of the looks above can be written with them: there's no token for a second row, a band behind the column heads, the table's gap color, a button's face, a link's hover, an input, or the scrollbar. The panel under the frame lists the roughly twenty-five a period skin needs. A token may hold a gradient, so a "GIF" header is still just a value. Second, the picker. A row of skewed radios works for three choices. It doesn't work for seven, and nobody chooses a skin by its name.

The tributes in the switcher (Blue Steel, Midnight, Hot Rod, Fan Site) are concepts: the default blue skin every 2002 board shipped, the navy dark skin of the vBulletin 3 years, the flames skin a fourteen-year-old made for their clan, and the classic blue of the fan-game board where this cast met. They're named for what they look like, not for the software they salute, and none uses another product's art. They'd ship as instance themes, so the engine never learns them. They also seed the player-made themes the screens design already promised: a submitted theme is one more file of the same tokens, checked by the same guard and the same contrast test.

The picker, sketched on the glass

Theme and mode split into two controls. Each tile is drawn from that theme's own tokens, read at build time from its file, so a preview can't drift from the theme. Choosing applies at once, as it does now. A theme that holds one mode greys the mode row and says why.

What this round asks

  • How strict the house stays: Strict, Silver, or something between.
  • The buttons: Plain, Bevel, or Chrome, and whether Chrome is the house's or only the themes'.
  • Themes: whether the token vocabulary and the picker are part of this design, and which tributes are worth drawing properly.

Once the look is settled, the screen boards follow in batches, as the glass's did: the index and a board; a thread with the composer; a profile and its record; the standings and the reveal; The System's profile and the move notice; the errors; and one phone board per batch. Each becomes a manifest the board match compares.
